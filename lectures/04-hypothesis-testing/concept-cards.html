Card 1 — the p-value, read off one picture

Both hills are the same thing: 10,000 worlds in which the group labels were shuffled at random, so only luck is at work. The red line is the difference we actually measured. Same warehouse, same week, same code, and two opposite verdicts.

The whole idea in one question: if these two groups really were one and the same, how often would chance alone hand me a gap this big? Build the world where they are the same, count how often it beats you, and that share is the p-value.
1Sectors 5761 vs 5130

medians 135.3 against 133.5 picks per hour, so a gap of +1.8

Shaded = 6,588 of 10,000 → p = 0.66 → nothing found. Two thirds of pure-luck worlds beat us.
2Sectors 5402 vs 5400

medians 149.9 against 113.3 picks per hour, so a gap of +36.5 (note the wider axis)

Shaded = 0 of 10,000 → p < 0.0001 → real. Write the <: with ten thousand worlds, "fewer than one in ten thousand" is the smallest number this machine can produce.
p = the share of luck-worlds whose difference is at least as big as ours. Nothing more, nothing less. Small p → luck is a poor explanation.
  • the probability that my hypothesis is true — it is a property of the luck-worlds, not of your belief
  • the probability that the result is due to chance — it assumes chance and asks how often chance does this
  • the importance of the effect — p measures surprise, not size
  • p = 0.06 means nothing is there — it means this evidence is not enough
  • p < 0.05 means the finding matters — with 150,000 rows, a 2% effect clears the bar

A large p-value is a failure to find a difference, not proof of sameness. And the bar (0.05, 0.01) is a convention agreed before looking, priced by what a false alarm costs you.

Card 2 — where that picture comes from

Card 1 built the luck distribution by shuffling. A statistics course reaches the same picture from the other end, and it is worth seeing both, because the second route explains why the first one works. Five steps, in order. Steps 1 to 4 use a made-up population of cats, because there you can see the truth and check every claim. Step 5 comes back to the warehouse. Each step is one slide's worth, so the sequence works on screen as well as on paper.

1One sample is not the population

I want the average weight of a cat. I cannot weigh every cat, so I weigh 100 of them.

The shape of my 100 looks like the shape of the population. The average still misses by 0.12 kg, and nothing in my data says so.
2What if I could do the whole study again?

Imagine 2,000 other people each weighing their own 100 cats. Their averages form a distribution of their own. Drag n and watch it.

two bands, one shared scale

This is the central limit theorem. It does not say averages drift towards the truth, they are centred on it from the start. What it gives you is the shape (a bell, whatever the population looks like) and the width: σ divided by the square root of n. Four times the cats, half the width.
3So I can say how wrong I probably am

Turn that width into a range around my own average and call it a confidence interval. Each bar below is one study's interval. The green line is the one true average, and it never moves.

Read the picture, not the slogan. The truth does not move; the bars do. "95% confident" is a statement about the bars: build them this way forever and 95% of them will cover the truth. It is not a 95% chance that this one bar is right, and it is not the range a single cat's weight falls in.
4And you do not need the formula at all

Step 2 imagined 2,000 other studies. I cannot run them. But I can treat my own 100 cats as a stand-in for the population and draw 100 out of them again and again, with replacement. That is the bootstrap.

Bootstrap interval 4.81 to 5.32, formula interval 4.80 to 5.32. Same answer, no algebra, and it works for medians, ratios and p90 where the formula runs out. Same trick as card 1's shuffle: when you cannot do the maths, simulate.
5The same machinery answers "two groups, or one?"

Now the warehouse, on the same pair of sectors as card 1. One thing changes here and it matters: card 1 shuffled medians, this panel shuffles means, because the mean is the summary the textbook formula covers. So the numbers below are not card 1's numbers, and they should not be: the gap of means is +3.5 picks per hour where the gap of medians was +1.8. The verdict is the same either way, which is the point. Here are the shuffle and the formula, drawn on top of each other.

Shuffling says the width is 3.90, the formula says 3.99. The p-values are 0.37 and 0.38, and card 1's shuffle of medians said 0.66. Three machines, one verdict: nothing found. A named test is not a different kind of evidence from the shuffle. It is the same picture, drawn by algebra instead of by brute force.
The whole chain in one line: an average is a random thing with a known width → so you can say how far the truth plausibly sits from your estimate → so you can ask how odd your gap would be if the two groups were really one. That last question is the p-value.
  • there is a 95% chance the true average is inside my interval — the truth does not move, the interval does. 95% of intervals built this way cover it (step 3)
  • 95% of cats weigh between 4.72 and 5.19 kg — that is roughly the range for the average of 100 cats. Single cats run 3.0 to 7.7 kg, about ten times wider (step 2)
  • a big p-value means the two groups are probably the same — it means a gap like ours is unremarkable when they are the same. Card 1, myth one
  • the central limit theorem says averages drift towards the truth — they are centred on it already. The theorem gives the shape and the width (step 2)

Measured, not asserted: over 2,000 simulated studies at n = 100, 94.7% of these intervals contained the true average, which is the 95% the method promises. Every drawn figure on this card comes from concept-cards-figures.ipynb. The two live panels build their own population in your browser, which is why their truth sits at 4.93 kg where the drawn ones say 4.94: two simulations of the same world, two slightly different answers. That gap is the whole subject of the card.

Card 3 — choosing a test without a PhD

Before choosing one, it helps to see that they are all the same procedure. Every test on these cards is these five steps, and a named test is just steps 2 and 3 solved in advance.

  1. A claim worth disbelieving. "These two groups are really one group." That is the null hypothesis.
  2. One number that would be large if the claim were false. A difference of means, a difference of medians, a pile of counts. That is the test statistic.
  3. The spread that number would have if the claim were true. Shuffle the labels yourself (card 1), or let a formula tell you (card 2, step 5).
  4. Where your real number lands in that spread. The share of that world at least as extreme as yours: the p-value.
  5. A bar you agreed on before looking, and a verdict that carries the effect size next to it.

So the tests below differ in exactly two places: which number they use for step 2, and how they get the spread in step 3. Nothing else.

Comparing two groups of numberstimes, amounts, prices, durations
▶
stats.ttest_ind(a, b)
Start here. It compares the two means, it is one line, and it is what everyone else will have run. At the sample sizes we work with it behaves itself even on a skewed column, because an average of thousands of values is well behaved whatever the values look like (card 2, step 2).
…and the column has a long taila few values hundreds of times the typical one
▶
stats.mannwhitneyu(a, b)
A second opinion, run beside the t-test rather than instead of it. It puts every value in order and compares ranks, so one twenty-five-minute pause counts once. Reach for it when the mean stops describing the typical case: on our pick times the mean is 11.1 s and the typical pick is 6.6 s.
Comparing shares of yes/no countserror rates, conversion, defect share
▶
stats.chi2_contingency(table)
Feed it a contingency table of raw counts, one row per group, one column per outcome. Never percentages. Inside, it pools both groups into one rate, works out how many wrong scans each group would get at that shared rate, and adds up how far the real counts sit from those expectations.
Comparing a number against a numbertask size against pace, weight against time
▶
cut it into groups first
None of the tests above take two columns of numbers. Turn one of them into groups (big tasks against small ones, heavy against light) and you are back on this map. Where you cut is a decision you must defend: a split at the median asks a different question from a split at the top quartile, and "no effect" can simply mean "no effect the way I first cut it". This is the shape of HW1 Part 2's first hypothesis.
Not sure / the metric is unusualmedians, ratios, p90, anything home-made
▶
permutation test
Shuffle the labels 10,000 times and count. Always legal, works for any metric you can compute, and you can explain it to a manager in one sentence.

And when the first two disagree? That is information, not a menu. They summarise the column differently, so a wide gap between them means the two summaries tell different stories. Night against day in demo 3: the t-test says p = 0.38 and the mean is 1.3% slower at night, the rank test says p = 0.00004 and the median is 2.0% faster. Nothing is broken. The groups are simply so close that the choice of summary decides the answer, which is itself the verdict. Report both, and never go shopping for the friendlier one.

The test must match the data, and the number you report must match the decision. Scipy spells them all in one line each — choosing what to report is the skill, not typing.
And no test at all is legal before you have answered the earlier question: does my metric measure the thing I mean? See card 3.

Two more habits that travel with the choice: report the effect size next to the p-value, and name what else differs between the groups (the confounder check).

Card 5 — choosing a test: the fuller map

Card 3 is the working map for this course. This one is the reference you will want later, when the data stops being two independent piles of numbers. Extra: nothing on this card is examined, and none of it is needed for HW1.

1Paired or not is the first fork, and it is about the design, not the data

Unpaired: group A and group B are different people, different sites, different warehouses. Paired: the same unit measured twice, before and after; or a switchback experiment where you deliberately match Monday to Monday and Tuesday to Tuesday. Pairing is something you built into the experiment, so you know the answer before you look at a single number.

Getting this wrong costs you either a real effect or a fake one. A paired test on unpaired data is not a stricter version of the same thing; it is a different question.
2"The data must be normal" is the most common myth in the box

What has to behave is the average, not the data. That is why the boxes above say AVERAGE in capitals. Our own pick times are a good example: the column itself is wildly skewed, and at the sample sizes we use the average of it is textbook normal.

But "n > 30 and you are fine" is a rule of thumb, not a law, and how large n has to be depends on how skewed the column is. Measured on our pick times (skewness 22.6), two identical groups and a 5% bar: at n = 30 the test fires on 2.2% of runs, at n = 1,000 on 4.2%. It is not lying to you, it is being too cautious, which costs you real findings rather than inventing fake ones. One-sided tests suffer more than two-sided ones.
3The "equal variances?" branch is one you can delete

Many charts put a Levene test here and send you to Student or to Welch depending on the answer. Measured, two identical populations, 5% bar, group sizes 10 and 40 with one spread three times the other:

routefires onshould be
plain ttest_ind0.2%5%
Levene first, then choose4.4%5%
equal_var=False always4.7%5%
Welch costs nothing when the variances really are equal and saves you when they are not. So skip the branch and the extra test: pass equal_var=False every time. Note that scipy does not do this for you, its default is the plain version.
4Does your test actually work here? Run it on two identical groups

Split one population in half at random and test. There is no effect by construction, so the test should fire on exactly α of the runs, and its p-values should be spread evenly between 0 and 1. That is what "an A/A test" means, and `stats.kstest` against a uniform will tell you whether they are.

One trap in that check. Even spreading is a property of tests built on a continuous number. On counts it fails by design: chi-square on 40 observations per group came out with an average p of 0.69 and fired on 1.5% of runs instead of 5%. The test is not broken, it is discrete and cautious. A failed uniformity check on a counting test is not, by itself, bad news.
5And in production, most of this stops mattering

With tens of thousands of rows the average is well behaved whatever the column looks like, Welch handles the variances, and the choice between the boxes above changes the decimals rather than the verdict.

What does not stop mattering is everything on card 1's checklist: the more data you have, the less the shape of it matters and the more the design does. At big n everything clears the bar, so the binding question stops being "is it real" and becomes "is it big enough to act on, and did I measure the right thing at all". That is card 4.

A naming trap worth knowing. wilcoxon in scipy is the paired rank test. The unpaired one is mannwhitneyu, which older books call the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Same surname, two different tests, and they sit on opposite sides of the chart above.
